# Evaluation: A 30-Day Blanket Ban on Political Demonstrations

## Conclusion

On the facts given, the restriction is **not justified in its current form**. The government has a legitimate aim and a lawful process, but the measure fails the test its own governing philosophy sets: it is not narrowly tailored to the risk it identifies. A targeted response to the one credible threat would very likely have achieved the same safety goal at far lower cost to civil liberties.

## The government's strongest case

- **Legitimate aim.** Preventing political violence is a real state duty, and constitutional liberalism has never treated rights as unlimited.
- **Credible evidence.** The risk at one demonstration is described as credible, not speculative.
- **Procedural regularity.** The measure went through an established legal process and is time-limited.
- **Reviewability.** Independent courts can still examine it, which distinguishes it from emergency rule by decree.
- **Practical argument.** Officials may argue that policing capacity is finite, that narrower bans are hard to administer, or that threats can migrate between events. These are plausible concerns, but the scenario offers no evidence for them.

## Where the justification weakens

**1. Mismatch between evidence and scope.** The evidence concerns *one* planned event. No evidence shows comparable risk elsewhere. A restriction on all demonstrations treats the many as proxies for the one, which is the core defect.

**2. The threat is non-imminent.** Time exists to use ordinary tools: targeted intelligence work, event-specific conditions, rerouting, additional policing, or prosecuting incitement and planning. Emergency-grade measures are hardest to defend when ordinary ones haven't been tried or explained away.

**3. Peaceful assembly is the baseline.** Under constitutional liberalism, the burden falls on the state to show necessity and proportionality. Peaceful, unrelated gatherings should not have to justify themselves.

**4. Selective burden on dissent.** A blanket ban on *political* demonstrations lands hardest on the people with the fewest alternative channels, including opposition groups, minorities, and those protesting the government itself. Even if the intent is benign, a government restricting dissent against itself has a conflict of interest that deserves heightened scrutiny.

**5. Chilling effect.** A 30-day ban can suppress mobilization well beyond 30 days, since organizing, permits, and momentum are hard to restore.

**6. Precedent risk.** Accepting a broad ban on a narrow justification lowers the threshold for the next one.

## Less restrictive alternatives

- Prohibit or condition **only the specific high-risk event**, with reasons given.
- Impose time, place, and manner limits (route changes, buffer zones, staggered schedules) on that event.
- Apply an **individualized-risk approach**: restrict specific organizers or participants where evidence supports it, rather than all participants.
- Increase policing and de-escalation resources for the flagged event.
- Pursue criminal enforcement against those planning violence.
- If any broader measure is needed, apply it **regionally or by event category**, supported by stated risk criteria.

## Appropriate safeguards (if any restriction proceeds)

- **Sunset clause** with renewal requiring fresh evidence, not automatic extension.
- **Expedited judicial review** with the government bearing the burden of showing necessity, and access to the underlying evidence (security-cleared if needed).
- **Legislative oversight** and periodic public reporting on the threat assessment.
- **Narrowing triggers:** automatic exemption for events assessed as low-risk.
- **Non-discrimination:** viewpoint-neutral application, with monitoring for selective enforcement.
- **Preserved alternative channels** for expression (press, petitions, online speech).
- **Post-hoc review** and remedies for people wrongly affected.

## Legal authorization vs. democratic legitimacy

These are related but distinct.

- **Legal authorization** asks: was the power exercised through valid procedure within the law? Here, apparently yes.
- **Democratic legitimacy** asks: does the exercise of power respect the conditions that make majority rule worth honoring, namely free expression, open contestation, accountability, and equal standing for dissenters?

A measure can be fully lawful yet corrosive to legitimacy. Elections confer authority to govern, not a license to suspend the channels through which that authority is challenged. Under constitutional liberalism, an electoral mandate does not by itself justify a restriction. The restriction must also pass the tests of necessity and proportionality. Here, lawfulness and judicial availability are necessary conditions for legitimacy but not sufficient ones. Notably, the government's own philosophy supplies the standard by which this measure falls short, so the critique is internal rather than imposed from outside.

## Remaining uncertainty

- **Nature of the threat.** "Credible" and "non-imminent" leave open how severe, how well-sourced, and whether it could spread to other events.
- **Government's reasons.** There may be classified information or capacity constraints the scenario doesn't reveal.
- **Judicial review in practice.** Availability isn't the same as speed or effectiveness. If courts rule after 30 days, review is moot.
- **Context.** Recent political violence, the government's record on dissent, and whether the ban coincides with politically sensitive events would all shift the assessment.
- **Alternatives' feasibility.** I've assumed targeted measures were workable; if they weren't, the case for a broader measure improves, though still not to the point of an all-event ban.
- **Normative disagreement.** Reasonable people weigh security against liberty differently, and some would accept broader restrictions after recent violence. My conclusion reflects the constitutional-liberal standard specified in the scenario.

## Bottom line

The aim is legitimate and the process is lawful, but proportionality fails. The evidence supports a narrow response to a specific risk, and the government chose a broad one. As an advisory matter, I'd recommend the government **withdraw or substantially narrow the restriction**, targeting the identified event and preserving everyone else's right to assemble, while keeping judicial review meaningful and time-bound.
